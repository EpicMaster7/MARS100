EarthMarsTransfer
Kepler meets the rocket equation

A minimum-energy path, not a straight line.

Earth and Mars orbit the Sun at different speeds. An idealized transfer ellipse intersects Earth's orbit at departure and Mars' orbit at arrival. The spacecraft must depart when Mars is roughly 44° ahead.

This uses the ideal Keplerian transfer ellipse and circular planetary orbits. Dates, perturbations, trajectory correction burns, atmosphere and precision landing are excluded.

06 · Model assumptions

  • Mars EDL is the biggest unknown. Aerobraking is assumed and a fixed 0.60 km/s terminal burn is imposed. The analysis does not simulate heating, aerodynamics, landing engines, gravity loss, or terrain hazards.
  • Cost and success probabilities are configurable scenario parameters, not sourced launch prices or mission reliability forecasts. The assumptions support comparative reasoning only.
  • Mass budgets are coarse. Tanks, TPS, attitude control, docking hardware, cryogenic storage and propellant boiloff are not independently sized. Their omissions can reverse feasibility.
  • Independence is optimistic. Six spacecraft can share a defect, weather conditions, launch infrastructure, or mission-control failures. Correlated failures would reduce the reliability benefit of redundancy.
  • Time matters. The six-lander architecture is assumed to use two Mars launch opportunities, delaying the final delivery by approximately 26 months relative to a one-window campaign.
